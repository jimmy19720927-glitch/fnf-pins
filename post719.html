Citrulline is in almost every pre-workout on the shelf, usually near the top of the label, usually in a dose nobody bothers to justify. The pitch is always the same: more nitric oxide, more blood flow, more pump, more reps. The research is more interesting than the pitch — and less flattering. The honest summary of the citrulline malate benefits literature is that the effect is real, consistently small, and shows up in a narrower set of situations than the marketing suggests.

Below: what citrulline does mechanically, what the two big meta-analyses actually measured, the trials that found nothing at all, why the “malate” part probably does not matter, and how to dose it if you decide it is worth your money.

What citrulline actually does in your body

Citrulline is a non-essential amino acid, and its job in this context is to become something else. Your kidneys convert it into arginine, and arginine is the raw material your body uses to make nitric oxide — the signalling molecule that relaxes blood vessel walls and lets more blood through.

The obvious question is why anyone bothers with the detour. Why not just take arginine? Because arginine is a bad oral supplement. A 2008 pharmacokinetic study in British Journal of Clinical Pharmacology put 20 healthy volunteers through six different dosing regimens of placebo, citrulline and arginine, and found that oral citrulline raised plasma arginine levels more effectively than oral arginine itself (PMID: 17662090). Arginine gets chewed up by intestinal arginase before it reaches your bloodstream. Citrulline slips past that and gets converted downstream. At 3 g twice daily, citrulline also raised urinary nitrate and cGMP, the standard markers that nitric oxide signalling actually went up.

So the mechanism is not in dispute. Citrulline reliably does the biochemical thing it claims to do. Whether that biochemical thing moves the number on the bar is a separate question, and that is where it gets messy.

Citrulline malate benefits: what the trials actually found

The study everyone cites is from 2010. Pérez-Guisado and Jakeman put 41 trained men through two identical chest sessions — eight sets of bench press to failure at 80% of 1RM — once with 8 g of citrulline malate, once with placebo, double-blind and crossover (PMID: 20386132). Reps went up from the third set onward, with a 52.9% increase on the final set, and self-reported soreness at 24 and 48 hours dropped by roughly 40%.

Those are enormous numbers, and they are the reason citrulline ended up in every pre-workout formula on earth. They are also, fifteen years later, an outlier. When researchers pooled everything that came after, the picture shrank considerably.

Reps to failure: about three extra reps

Vårvik and colleagues ran a meta-analysis in International Journal of Sport Nutrition and Exercise Metabolism covering eight double-blind placebo-controlled studies and 137 participants (PMID: 34010809). Taking 6–8 g of citrulline malate 40–60 minutes before training increased total repetitions to failure by 3 ± 5 reps — a 6.4% improvement, statistically significant at p = .022, with a small standardised effect size of 0.196.

Worth noting the subgroup split: lower body showed an 8.1% improvement that just missed significance (p = .051), and upper body showed 5.7% that did not reach it (p = .131). The overall effect held; the neat story about where it works did not.

A separate meta-analysis in Sports Med by Trexler and colleagues pooled 12 studies and 198 participants across strength and power outcomes and landed almost exactly on the same number: a significant benefit (p = 0.036) with a pooled effect size of 0.20 (PMID: 30895562). The authors were candid about it — every individual study's confidence interval crossed zero, and the effect only became visible once they were stacked. Their conclusion was that this size of effect is relevant to athletes whose competitions are decided by small margins, which is a polite way of saying it is not going to change your Tuesday.

Soreness and perceived effort: the more consistent finding

Rhim and colleagues pooled 13 randomised trials and 206 participants looking specifically at how training felt (PMID: 33308806). Citrulline significantly reduced rating of perceived exertion (p = 0.03) and muscle soreness at 24 hours (p = 0.04). It did nothing for soreness at 72 hours and did not lower blood lactate at all — which quietly kills the “citrulline buffers lactic acid” claim that still shows up on labels.

This is arguably the more useful finding. A supplement that makes hard sets feel slightly less hard is a supplement that helps you finish the session you planned, which compounds over months in a way three extra reps does not. It is the same logic behind caffeine's effect on perceived exertion.

Endurance: a small, real time-trial effect

Suzuki and colleagues gave 22 trained men 2.4 g/day of L-citrulline for seven days, then 2.4 g one hour before a 4 km cycling time trial (PMID: 26900386). Completion time improved and the power-output-to-oxygen-cost ratio went up, alongside higher plasma nitrite and nitrate. A 2015 study in Journal of Applied Physiology found the same pattern from a different angle: L-citrulline sped up oxygen uptake kinetics and improved high-intensity exercise tolerance (PMID: 26023227).

The trials that found nothing

Any honest read has to include these, because there are more of them than the supplement aisle admits.

Chappell and colleagues ran 19 trained participants through German Volume Training — ten sets of ten barbell curls at 80% 1RM, which is about as brutal a fatigue protocol as you can design — with 8 g of citrulline malate or placebo (PMID: 30458655). No effect on total reps at all. Median 57 reps on citrulline versus 61 on placebo. No difference in blood lactate or creatine kinase. Soreness was significantly reduced, which lines up with the meta-analysis above, but the performance claim died in that study.

A larger and more recent trial is harder to wave away. Martín-Olmedo and colleagues tested 43 trained men and women (21 women) on placebo, 5.3 g L-citrulline, and citrulline malate, measuring maximal neuromuscular performance, countermovement jump, and strength-endurance across squat and bench press (PMID: 39662304). Nothing improved. Not maximal strength, not ballistic performance, not total reps, not perceived exertion. Every p-value sat at .061 or above.

Notice the volume difference. The 2010 study that produced the huge effect used eight sets to failure. The 2024 study that found nothing used three sets of ten — described by the authors as low-to-moderate volume. The pattern across the literature is that citrulline shows up when the session is long enough to accumulate real fatigue, and disappears when it is not.

L-citrulline vs citrulline malate: does the malate matter?

Citrulline malate is citrulline bound to malic acid, usually in a 2:1 ratio. Malate is a Krebs cycle intermediate, and the theory has always been that it contributes something to energy production on its own. The theory is now testable, and it has been tested twice.

The 2024 crossover trial above compared L-citrulline and citrulline malate head to head at matched citrulline doses. Neither beat placebo, and neither beat the other. Bayat and colleagues then ran a six-week chronic study in 33 resistance-trained men on 8 g L-citrulline, 12 g citrulline malate, or placebo alongside a training programme (PMID: 40470618). Both citrulline groups beat placebo on upper-body reps to failure and both raised post-exercise nitric oxide metabolites — and there was no apparent difference between the two forms.

Practical translation: buy on citrulline content per dollar, not on the word malate. And read the ratio — 8 g of citrulline malate 2:1 delivers roughly 5.3 g of actual citrulline, not 8. That single detail explains a lot of the inconsistency between studies.

How to dose it

  • Dose: 6–8 g citrulline malate, or 3–6 g pure L-citrulline. Anything under 3 g was excluded from the Trexler meta-analysis for good reason.
  • Timing: 40–60 minutes before training. That is the window used across the pooled studies, and it matches how long absorption and conversion take.
  • Loading: not required for the acute effect, but the endurance and six-week trials both used daily dosing. If you are chasing the time-trial effect, take it every day, not just on training days.
  • Where it earns its place: high-volume, multi-set, close-to-failure work. If your session is three sets of five, skip it.
  • Stacking: it does not conflict with anything standard. Most people are already getting some in their pre-workout — check the label before you double up.

Unflavoured citrulline malate tastes genuinely sour. Mixing it into a flavoured drink rather than plain water is not a preference thing, it is a compliance thing.

Who should skip it

If you are new to training and building a shelf from scratch, citrulline is not where the money goes first. An effect size of 0.20 sits well below protein, creatine and sleep, and it belongs at the end of a sensible beginner supplement stack, not the front. It is in the same tier as beta-alanine — a real but modest edge for people who have already fixed the things that matter more.

Skip it too if your training is low-volume and strength-focused, if you are already getting 6–8 g from a fully dosed pre-workout, or if you were expecting the 52.9% number from 2010 to be your experience. It will not be.

The bottom line

Stripped of the label copy, the citrulline malate benefits worth paying for are narrow and honest. Citrulline does what it says biochemically, and the pooled evidence supports a small real effect: roughly three extra reps across a high-volume session, lower perceived exertion, and less soreness at 24 hours. It does not buffer lactate, the malate does not appear to add anything, and it vanishes entirely in short low-volume sessions.

That is a supplement worth twenty dollars if you train with volume and you want the last two sets to feel like the first two. It is not worth reorganising your training around, and it is not worth buying inside a proprietary blend at four times the price.

Frequently asked questions

How long before a workout should I take citrulline?

40 to 60 minutes. That is the window used in the studies pooled by Vårvik, and it allows time for absorption and conversion to arginine.

Is citrulline malate better than plain L-citrulline?

Two direct comparisons — one acute crossover in 43 trained adults, one six-week trial in 33 trained men — found no difference between the forms at matched citrulline doses. Buy whichever gives you more citrulline per dollar.

Does citrulline have side effects?

It is well tolerated in the trials at these doses. The most common complaint is mild stomach discomfort from taking a large unflavoured dose on an empty stomach. Speak to a doctor before using it if you take blood pressure medication, since it acts on the same vasodilation pathway.

Do I need to take citrulline every day?

Not for the resistance-training effect, which is acute. The endurance benefits were produced with seven days of daily dosing, so if that is what you are after, take it daily.
